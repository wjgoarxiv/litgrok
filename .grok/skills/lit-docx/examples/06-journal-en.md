# Background

Rail operators and publishers both like to say that commuters read. The claim is usually based on what people report in a single survey question, and it rarely separates a glance at headlines from forty minutes with a novel. We wanted a closer look at what reading on a train actually consists of, minute by minute, and at the conditions that let a short scroll turn into sustained attention.

The line we chose runs 38 km from a dormitory suburb into the city centre, with eleven stops and a typical morning journey of 34 to 52 minutes. Trains between 07:00 and 08:30 run every six minutes and are usually full by the fourth stop. That crowding made the line a useful place to ask whether a seat matters as much as riders say it does.

## Questions

We set out with three questions. What share of morning trips includes any reading, and what share includes long-form reading? Which conditions of the trip go with long-form reading? And how do people who read regularly on the train describe what they do?

# Method

## Participants

We recruited through posters at four stations and a notice in the operator's newsletter. Of 97 volunteers, 42 met the criteria: a weekday commute on the line of at least 25 minutes, at least four trips a week, and no job that required reading on the train. The group was 24 women and 18 men, aged 23 to 64 (median 38). Thirty-one boarded at one of the first four stations.

## The diary

Each participant used a short form on their phone at the end of every morning trip. The form asked for boarding and alighting times, whether they found a seat and when, what they read, on which device, and for roughly how many minutes. A free-text box at the end invited any comment. Participants filled in the form for ten consecutive working days in March 2026 and received a book voucher on completion.

We defined long-form reading as at least ten continuous minutes with a single book, article or report. Messages, social feeds, news headlines and work email counted as reading but not as long-form reading. We checked each diary against the operator's timetable to flag entries that did not fit the journey, and removed 9 of 770 trip records for that reason.

## Interviews

After the diary period we interviewed 12 participants for about 30 minutes each, chosen to cover frequent, occasional and non-readers. The interviews followed a short guide about routines, interruptions and what made a trip feel well spent.

# Findings

## How much reading

Reading of some kind appeared on 634 of 761 trips (83 %). Long-form reading appeared on 236 trips (31 %). The rest of the reading was short: feeds, messages and news, often in bursts of two or three minutes between stations. Table 1 sets out the mix.

Table 1. Reading on 761 morning trips (sample data)

| Type of reading | Trips | Share |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Any reading | 634 | 83 % |
| Long-form, 10 min or more | 236 | 31 % |
| Short reading only | 398 | 52 % |
| No reading | 127 | 17 % |

Books made up 58 % of long-form sessions, followed by long articles (27 %) and work reports (15 %). Paper books were more common than e-readers among those who read books, by 61 to 39.

## Conditions that go with long-form reading

The seat mattered most. Riders who sat down within the first ten minutes read long-form on 46 % of trips, against 12 % for those who stood for longer or the whole way. Trip length had a smaller effect: among seated riders, trips over 45 minutes reached 51 %, trips under 40 minutes 39 %.

Table 2. Long-form reading by seat and trip length (sample data)

| Seat in first 10 min | Under 40 min | 40–45 min | Over 45 min |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Yes | 39 % | 47 % | 51 % |
| No | 9 % | 12 % | 15 % |

Device made little difference once the seat was taken into account. Age showed no clear pattern. Boarding at the first four stations raised the chance of a seat, which is why early boarders read more, and the effect disappeared when we compared early and late boarders who both found a seat.

::: callout kind=note title="Reading the shares"
The shares in Tables 1 and 2 are counted over trips, not people. A few frequent readers contribute many trips, so the person-level picture in Section 3.3 is the safer guide to how common the habit is.
:::

## Who reads regularly

Fourteen participants read long-form on at least six of their ten mornings. Eighteen did so on one to five mornings, and ten never did. The frequent readers were not younger or older than the rest, and their journeys were only four minutes longer on average.

# What the diaries suggest

The interviews add a pattern the numbers only hint at. Frequent readers planned. They chose the book or article the evening before, downloaded it if needed, and several said they let a train go by if the next one looked less crowded. One described the first half hour of the day as "the only part nobody else can book". Occasional readers spoke of reading as something that happened when the conditions allowed; non-readers often said they would read if a seat were free, and the diaries show it rarely was.

This matters for two groups. For operators, it suggests that extra seated capacity early in the line has a value beyond comfort, because a seat changes what people do with the trip. For publishers and libraries, it suggests that reading on the move depends on preparation, and that tools which help someone choose and download the next thing to read the night before may reach commuters better than offers delivered on the platform.

# Limits of the study

The sample is small, self-selected and drawn from one line. Volunteers who answer a poster about reading are likely to read more than the average rider, so the overall shares probably overstate reading among commuters at large. Diaries rely on memory a few minutes after the trip, and minutes of reading are estimates. The study ran for two weeks in March, before the longer evenings and lighter trains of spring. A repeat with a random sample, on more than one line and in more than one season, would test whether the seat effect holds.
